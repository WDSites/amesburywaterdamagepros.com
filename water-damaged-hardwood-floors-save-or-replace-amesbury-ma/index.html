Water Damaged Hardwood Floors in Amesbury, MA: Save or Replace?

Wet boards can often be dried and kept if the work starts early, and this page shows how to tell which floors are worth saving.

By the Amesbury Water Damage Pros team · Published 2026-10-29

Call (978) 252-8637

Most water damaged hardwood floors can be saved if the water was clean, the boards were wet for a short time and drying started within the first 48 to 72 hours. After that window, boards swell harder, finish starts to fail and mold gets a foothold underneath. The call comes down to three things: how long the floor stayed wet, what kind of water it was and what the floor is sitting on.

If your floor is wet right now, stop the source, soak up what you can with towels and call (978) 252-8637. Dispatch is open 24/7, and the sooner a crew reads the moisture in your boards, the more options you keep.

What decides whether a wet floor survives

Solid hardwood is a natural sponge. It takes on water through the seams and the underside, swells across the grain and then tries to dry from the top. That uneven drying is what bends the boards.

Water category matters first. Clean water from a supply line or a roof leak is a very different problem from gray water out of a dishwasher or sewage from a backed-up drain. Category 2 and 3 water means contaminated material has to come out, and boards soaked with sewage are not kept.

The second factor is time. A floor wet for a few hours is a different job from a floor wet for four days. Third is construction. Solid planks over a wood subfloor behave differently from engineered boards, which have a thin wood layer glued over plywood or fiberboard cores that can delaminate.

The first 48 to 72 hours

What you do before the crew arrives matters, and so does what you leave alone.

  1. Stop the water.Shut the valve or contain the leak. If the source is a roof or window, a tarp or plastic buys time.
  2. Pull everything off the floor.Rugs, boxes and furniture trap moisture and stain the finish. Put foil or plastic under furniture legs you cannot move.
  3. Remove standing water.Mops, towels and a wet vacuum work for small spills. A crew uses extraction units for anything larger.
  4. Run air, but do not bake the floor.Open windows if the air outside is drier and use fans. Do not point space heaters at the boards, because fast drying on top splits the wood.
  5. Get readings.A moisture meter tells you how wet the boards and subfloor are, and a thermal camera shows where water traveled under the surface.

Do not sand, refinish or seal a floor that has not been proven dry. Finishing over damp wood traps the moisture and the problem returns under a fresh coat.

Reading cupping, crowning and gaps

The shape of the boards tells you what happened and what comes next. Cupping is when the edges of each board rise higher than the center, which means the underside got wetter than the top. Crowning is the reverse, with a raised center, and it often shows up after a cupped floor was sanded too soon. Gaps between boards usually appear after drying, when swollen boards shrink back.

Mild cupping often relaxes as the floor dries, and many floors flatten out over a few weeks. Sharp cupping that does not ease, boards lifting off the subfloor or a floor that feels spongy points toward replacement. Black staining at the seams or a musty smell from the cracks means moisture has been sitting for a while, and that area needs a closer look before anything is saved.

Dry in place or tear out

Drying in place is the first choice when the floor is solid, the water was clean and the damage is recent. A crew sets air movers and LGR dehumidifiers for several days, and may use drying mats or panels that pull moisture out through the boards. Readings are logged each visit until the boards and subfloor match a dry reference reading.

Tear-out starts when the floor cannot reach that standard. Crews may remove a few boards to ventilate the cavity underneath, or pull the full floor when the subfloor is saturated, the water was contaminated or the boards are glued down over a wet slab. Removal is also how a hidden pocket of water gets found. Work follows the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard for water damage, so ask any company whether it does.

What the job costs

No honest price comes from a photo. Cost depends on the water category, how many square feet are wet, the species and thickness of the boards, how many days of drying equipment run, and the rebuild scope afterward. Scope and price are explained before work starts.

Drying in place usually ends with sanding and a new finish. A floor that is torn out needs new material, installation, staining and finish.

Insurance basics

Sudden and accidental water damage, such as a burst pipe or failed supply line, is often covered by a homeowners policy. Gradual leaks often are not, and flood damage needs separate flood coverage. Check your policy and ask your insurer. Photograph the floor, the source and the moisture readings before anything is removed.

Hardwood floor questions

How long can hardwood floors stay wet before they are ruined?

There is no single cutoff. Clean water that is dried within 48 to 72 hours leaves many floors in good shape. After several days, swelling, finish failure and mold under the boards become likely. A moisture meter on your floor gives the real answer.

Will cupped floors flatten on their own?

Mild cupping often eases as the boards dry, though it can take weeks. Do not sand until readings show the wood is dry, or the floor may crown later. Sharp cupping or lifting boards usually means replacement.

Can engineered hardwood be saved after a leak?

Sometimes, if it was wet briefly and the layers have not separated. Edges that peel or a floor that feels soft suggest it has delaminated and the boards should be replaced.

Should I pull up the boards myself?

Leave that to a crew unless you have to. Removing boards in the wrong order can damage ones that would have been saved, and a crew will want to see the layout and moisture readings first.
