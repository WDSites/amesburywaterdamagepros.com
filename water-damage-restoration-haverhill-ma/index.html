Haverhill neighborhoods and the Merrimack

Haverhill is the largest city on our service map, with 67,787 residents at the 2020 Census, and it spreads along the north bank of the Merrimack River from downtown and Washington Square out to Ward Hill and the I-495 exits. Bradford, annexed in 1897, sits across the river and still reads as its own neighborhood. The water problems differ from one end to the other.

Downtown and the river flats deal with the Merrimack and the small streams that feed it. Out toward Winnekenni Castle, a city park of about 700 acres that includes Kenoza Lake, the ground rises and the issue is more often an old roof or a basement than river water. Lake Saltonstall, known locally as Plug Pond, and the conservation land around it sit on a different drainage again.

Five I-495 exits and Routes 97, 108, 110, 113 and 125 run through the city, which means a crew's route from Amesbury depends on where you live. Tell dispatch your street and the nearest main road.

When water is spreading in Haverhill

If the water is near an outlet, a panel or a gas appliance, stay out and call the utility or 911 first. Sewage, river water and long-standing basement water are Category 3 under the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard, and porous materials they touched usually have to be removed.

What the record says about Merrimack floods

The National Weather Service says the record Merrimack River crests set in 1936 at Lowell, Lawrence and Haverhill still stand, and that the 1938 hurricane produced one of the three highest crests on the lower Merrimack from Lowell to Haverhill. Those are the benchmark floods for this part of the river, and they are the reason flood maps matter to riverside owners today.

Closer to the city center, a state case study describes the Little River Dam in central Haverhill, north of Winter Street near Stevens Street. The dam removal project aims to lower flood elevations in the nearby neighborhood, and the study says peak storm flows are projected to be 48 percent higher in 50 years. The city received a dam safety permit for the work in 2023.

If your home sits near the Little River or the Merrimack, check the FEMA flood map for your address and ask your insurer about flood coverage. Standard homeowners policies do not cover flood, according to the Massachusetts Division of Insurance. Our flood damage restoration page covers claims and cleanup in detail.

City housing and what it means for repairs

An American Community Survey summary puts Haverhill's median home build year at 1963, with about 37 percent of units built before 1940. That mix gives you everything from mill-era triple-deckers and brick blocks to 1960s capes and newer subdivisions in Ward Hill. Each fails in its own way: old drain stacks in the first, flat-roof leaks in the second, slab and crawl space moisture in the third.

Homes built before 1978 may have lead paint, and the EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting rule applies to paid work that disturbs paint. Ask any contractor whether their firm follows it before tearing out plaster or trim.

Once a structure is dry, water damage repair covers drywall, flooring and paint. If mold followed a slow leak, mold remediation explains containment and removal before any rebuild.

Permits in Haverhill

Haverhill is one of the few places on our map where the city's permit page is specific. The Inspectional Services Department, at City Hall on Summer Street, says permits are required for replacing drywall, for kitchen and bath remodeling and for flooring on a subfloor. Painting and rugs are cosmetic and need no permit, and electrical alterations need a permit. Plumbing and gas work needs a permit before work starts and a Massachusetts-licensed plumber.

The city page does not mention water damage specifically, so the safe move is to call Inspectional Services about your rebuild. That matters because a repair after a flood often involves replacing drywall, which the city lists as permit work.

Water and utilities

Haverhill's water is entirely surface water, according to the city's 2025 water quality report. Kenoza Lake is the primary source, fed by Millvale Reservoir and Crystal Lake, and the treatment plant sits on Amesbury Road. The report lists PFAS at an average of 3.09 parts per trillion, under the 20 limit, and lead at non-detect for the 90th percentile.

For a homeowner this means tap water is not usually a factor in a leak, but a break in a city water main can flood a street. Report main breaks to the Water Department, and tell dispatch if water is coming from outside.
